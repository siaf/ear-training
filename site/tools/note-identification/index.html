Which note reading game should I start with?

  • Treble clef notes if you play violin, flute, clarinet, sax, trumpet, guitar, or sing soprano or alto.
  • Bass clef notes for cello, double bass, bass guitar, bassoon, trombone, tuba and lower voices.
  • Grand staff notes for piano: both clefs at once, with middle C between them. Pair it with the piano keys game.
  • Alto clef notes for viola, and tenor clef notes for the high registers of cello, bassoon and trombone.
  • Guitar fretboard to learn where every note lives on the neck.

Each staff game starts small (just the lines, or the notes around middle C) and builds up to the whole staff, ledger lines and accidentals. Get 13 of your last 15 right and the next level unlocks. The mixed-clef game on this page is for once you know two or more clefs.

Why practise clefs mixed?

When you drill one clef at a time, your brain can stop looking at the clef. Mixing them forces the check every time, which is exactly what real music asks of a pianist moving between staves or a cellist switching from bass to tenor clef. Mixing is harder in the moment and tends to stick better; see interleaved practice for why.

Mnemonics get you started; landmarks make you fast. Learn where middle C and each clef's own reference line sit (G for treble, F for bass, C for alto and tenor), then read nearby notes by step or skip. The same sense of distance is what you hear in intervals.

Ledger lines and accidentals

Ledger lines are short lines added above or below the staff. They continue the line-space-line pattern, so count out from the nearest staff line. A sharp (♯) raises a note by a semitone and a flat (♭) lowers it; the letter still comes from the line or space. The games leave out E♯, B♯, F♭ and C♭, which are correct spellings but rare in beginner material. The circle of fifths shows which sharps and flats belong to each key.

Reading notes vs hearing them

Note reading tells you what a note is called. It doesn't tell you what it sounds like, which is why many good readers still can't play by ear or sing a line they haven't heard. That's ear training: hearing which note is higher, how far apart two notes are, and how each note relates to the key. Every game here plays the note when you answer to start linking the two; for the hearing side, try the interval ear trainer or read what is solfège.

Frequently asked questions

What is note identification?

Note identification means naming a written note from its position on the staff. It depends on the clef: the same line or space is a different note in treble, bass, alto and tenor clef. These games train it one clef at a time and then mixed.

Where is middle C on the staff?

One ledger line below the treble staff, one ledger line above the bass staff, the middle line of the alto clef and the fourth line of the tenor clef. On the grand staff it sits between the two staves.

What are ledger lines?

Short lines added above or below the five-line staff for notes outside it. They continue the line-space-line pattern of the staff, so you can read them by counting steps out from the nearest staff line.

Should I rely on mnemonics or landmark notes?

Use both, but aim to move past spelling out mnemonics. A few landmark notes, such as middle C and each clef's reference line, read by step or skip from there, are faster in real music than reciting a sentence for every note.

Which clef should I learn first?

The one your instrument reads. Treble clef for most high instruments and voices, bass clef for low instruments, both for piano, alto clef for viola. Add the others when your music needs them.
