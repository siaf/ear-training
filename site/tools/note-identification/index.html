How to use this note reading trainer

  • Read the note on the staff and tap its letter name, A to G (or press the letter on your keyboard). The note plays when you answer, so you start linking what you see to what you hear.
  • Pick a clef: treble, bass, or both mixed together. The clef name is shown above the staff as well as drawn on it.
  • Pick a range: On the staff covers the five lines, four spaces and the note just above and below. + Ledger lines adds up to two ledger lines above and below, including middle C.
  • Turn on accidentals when the naturals are easy. Choose ♭, ♮ or ♯ first, then tap the letter.
  • Each round is 20 notes. At the end you get your score and a list of the exact notes you missed.

Bass clef notes

The bass clef is the F clef: its two dots sit either side of the fourth line from the bottom, and that line is F (the F below middle C).

A common stumbling block is reading bass clef as if it were treble. The same position on the staff is a different note in each clef: the bottom line is E in treble but G in bass. If you play from both clefs, practice them mixed (the Both setting) so you're forced to check the clef every time.

Landmark notes: faster than mnemonics

Mnemonics get you started, but reciting "Every Good Boy…" for every note is slow. Most fluent readers work from a handful of landmark notes they recognize instantly, then read nearby notes by step (line to the next space) or skip (line to the next line):

  • Middle C: one ledger line below the treble staff, one ledger line above the bass staff.
  • Treble G: second line, where the clef curls.
  • Bass F: fourth line, between the clef's dots.
  • The outer lines: treble bottom line E and top line F; bass bottom line G and top line A.

Once those are automatic, a note one step above treble G is A, two steps below bass F is D, and so on. It's the same move-by-distance thinking you use to hear intervals, which is why reading and interval ear training reinforce each other.

Ledger lines and accidentals

Ledger lines are short extra lines for notes above or below the staff. They continue the line-space-line pattern, so count out from the nearest staff line. Middle C is the one to memorize first: the same pitch sits one ledger line below the treble staff and one above the bass staff, which is how the two staves connect in piano music.

A sharp (♯) raises a note by a semitone and a flat (♭) lowers it by a semitone. The letter name comes from the staff position; the accidental just modifies it, so a sharp on the treble G line is G♯. This trainer leaves out E♯, B♯, F♭ and C♭, which are correct spellings but rare in beginner material. Key signatures are a separate topic. The interactive circle of fifths shows which sharps or flats belong to each key.

How to practice note reading

  1. Accuracy first, then speed. Take the time you need to get each note right; speed comes from repetition, not from guessing.
  2. A few minutes every day. Short daily sessions beat an occasional long one. See spaced repetition for musicians.
  3. Say it, then hear it. Name the note out loud before you tap, then listen to the playback. If you have an instrument nearby, find the note on it too.
  4. Expand gradually. On-staff notes in one clef, then ledger lines, then the other clef, then mixed clefs, then accidentals.
  5. Read real music. Drills build recognition; simple sight-reading puts it to work.

Reading notes vs hearing them

Note reading tells you what a note is called. It doesn't tell you what it sounds like, which is why many people who read well still can't play by ear or sing a line they've never heard. That second skill is ear training: hearing which note is higher, how far apart two notes are, and how each note relates to the key. Frequently asked questions

What's the easiest way to remember the treble clef notes?

The lines from bottom to top are E, G, B, D, F ("Every Good Boy Does Fine"), and the spaces spell FACE. It also helps to know the treble clef's curl wraps around the second line, which is G.

How do I remember the bass clef notes?

Lines from bottom to top: G, B, D, F, A ("Good Boys Do Fine Always"). Spaces: A, C, E, G ("All Cows Eat Grass"). The two dots of the bass clef sit either side of the fourth line, which is F.

Where is middle C on the staff?

One ledger line below the treble staff, and one ledger line above the bass staff. On the grand staff it's the same note in both places, shared between the two clefs.

What are ledger lines?

Short lines added above or below the five-line staff for notes that fall outside it. They continue the staff's line-space-line pattern, so you can read them by counting steps out from the nearest staff line.

Should I rely on mnemonics or learn landmark notes?

Both, but aim to move beyond spelling out mnemonics. A few landmark notes (treble G, bass F, middle C), read by step or skip from there, are faster in real music than reciting a sentence for every note.
