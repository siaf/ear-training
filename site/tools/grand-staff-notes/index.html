Grand staff notes chart

The grand staff (also called the great staff) stacks a treble clef staff on top of a bass clef staff, joined on the left by a brace and a line. Together they cover the middle of the piano with only a few ledger lines. The two staves meet at middle C: one ledger line below the treble staff and one ledger line above the bass staff are the same note.

The same line or space is a different note on each staff: the bottom line is E on the top staff and G on the bottom staff. The usual mistake on the grand staff isn't forgetting a note, it's reading the bass staff with treble clef names. Glance at the clef, then read. There are full guides for each clef on the treble clef notes and bass clef notes pages.

Landmark notes across the grand staff

Pianists often learn the grand staff from a ladder of landmark notes, most of them Cs an octave apart, and read everything else by step or skip from the nearest one:

  • Low C (C2): two ledger lines below the bass staff.
  • Bass C (C3): the second space of the bass staff.
  • Bass F (F3): the fourth line of the bass staff, between the clef's dots.
  • Middle C (C4): the ledger line between the staves.
  • Treble G (G4): the second line of the treble staff, where the clef curls.
  • Treble C (C5): the third space of the treble staff.
  • High C (C6): two ledger lines above the treble staff.

Level 1 of the game drills six of these (C3, F3, middle C on both staves, G4 and C5). Once they're instant, levels 2 to 4 add every note on both staves, the ledger notes between them and accidentals.

Notes between the staves

The notes around middle C can be written on either staff. A3, B3 and C4 can sit on ledger lines below the treble staff; C4, D4 and E4 can sit on ledger lines above the bass staff. Which one the composer uses usually depends on which hand plays the note, and on keeping the lines easy to read. In the chart above, middle C is shown in both places. Level 3 of the game includes these in-between notes on both staves, which is often where piano reading slows down.

The right hand usually reads the treble staff and the left hand the bass staff, but that isn't a rule: either hand can take notes from either staff, and some passages put both hands in the same clef.

Who reads the grand staff?

Piano, keyboards, harp, celesta, marimba and other keyboard-like instruments. Organ music adds a third staff for the pedals. If you're learning piano, it also helps to know where each note is on the keyboard: try the piano keys game. And since reading tells you a note's name but not its sound, pair it with some interval ear training.

Frequently asked questions

What is the grand staff?

The grand staff is a treble clef staff and a bass clef staff joined by a brace and a line on the left. It is used for piano and other instruments with a wide range, and the two staves meet at middle C.

Where is middle C on the grand staff?

Between the two staves, on its own short ledger line. Written just below the treble staff or just above the bass staff, it is the same note: the C closest to the middle of a piano keyboard.

What are the notes on the grand staff?

Treble staff lines E G B D F and spaces F A C E; bass staff lines G B D F A and spaces A C E G. Together with the ledger notes between them they run continuously from the bottom of the bass staff to the top of the treble staff.

Why can the same note be written on both staves?

Notes around middle C (roughly A3 to E4) can be written with ledger lines below the treble staff or above the bass staff. Composers pick whichever is clearer, often based on which hand plays the note.

Does the right hand always play the treble staff?

Usually the right hand reads the top staff and the left hand the bottom staff, but it is not a rule. Either hand can play notes from either staff, and sometimes both staves use the same clef.
