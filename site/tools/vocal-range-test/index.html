How to take a vocal range test properly

Your result is only as good as the notes you give it. A few minutes of preparation makes the difference between a range that reflects your real voice and one that reflects a cold morning or a squeak you couldn't repeat.

  • Warm up first. Five minutes of humming, lip trills and gentle slides is enough. Cold voices lose notes at both ends. Our post on rhythmic vocal warm-ups has a routine you can borrow.
  • Find a quiet room and hold your phone or laptop at a normal distance. A TV or fan in the background makes pitch harder to read.
  • Sing on an open vowel like "ahh" and slide slowly. The test locks a note in only after you hold it steadily for about half a second, so sustained notes work better than quick jumps.
  • Stay comfortable. At the bottom, stop before the tone turns into vocal fry (a creaky rattle). At the top, stop before you strain, shout or flip into a thin squeak. Comfortable notes are what tell you about your voice type.
  • Test more than once. Run it on a couple of different days and go with the result you get consistently.

If a note won't register, it's usually too breathy, too short or too quiet. Sing a little louder on a clear "ahh", hold it, and move closer to the microphone.

Reading your result

The test reports notes in scientific pitch notation: a letter for the note and a number for the octave. Middle C is C4, and the number goes up at each C, so B3 is the note just below middle C. The lowest string on a standard-tuned guitar is E2; the A that orchestras tune to is A4. A range written as E2 – G4 runs from that low guitar E up to the G above middle C.

Range size is shown in octaves and semitones. One semitone is one key on a piano, white or black, and twelve semitones make an octave.

Voice types and their typical ranges

These are the conventional ranges used in choral and classical voice teaching. Real voices vary, and choral parts often use a narrower slice of each.

Voice typeTypical rangeNotes
BassE2 – E4Lowest common voice type.
BaritoneA2 – A4Between bass and tenor.
TenorC3 – C5Highest common male voice in chest voice.
Alto (contralto)F3 – F5Lowest common female voice type.
Mezzo-sopranoA3 – A5Between alto and soprano.
SopranoC4 – C6Highest common voice type.

Notice how much these overlap: a baritone and a tenor share most of their notes, as do a mezzo and a soprano. Countertenors, who sing in the alto range using a developed head voice, don't fit neatly into a range-only test.

Why your range isn't your voice type

Two singers can have the same lowest and highest notes and still be different voice types. Voice teachers listen for several things beyond range:

  • Tessitura: the part of your range where you can sing for a long time without tiring. A baritone may reach a tenor's high notes, but a tenor can live there.
  • Timbre: the weight and colour of the sound. Darker, heavier voices tend to be classified lower even with similar ranges.
  • Register transitions: where your voice naturally shifts gears (the passaggio). These shift points sit at different pitches for different voice types.

That's why this tool shows the closest voice types for your range and not a verdict. If you're choosing a choir part or repertoire, use the result as a starting point and ask a teacher or director to listen.

How to extend your range safely

Most people can unlock a few more usable notes, mostly at the top, by improving coordination, not by forcing. Your lowest notes are more limited by the size of your vocal folds, so expect less change there.

  • Use semi-occluded exercises. Lip trills, humming and singing through a straw let you slide through your range with less effort and strain.
  • Siren slowly from low to high and back on "ng" or "oo", aiming for smooth transitions between registers rather than higher peaks.
  • Move in small steps. Vocalises climb by half steps for a reason; see how vocalises are actually transposed.
  • Rest and hydrate. Short daily sessions beat one long push. Stop if anything hurts, and see a doctor if hoarseness lasts more than a couple of weeks.

Range is only half of singing well. The other half is hitting the notes you already have, in tune. Singing what you hear trains your ear and voice together, which is why vocalizing while you ear-train pays off. Frequently asked questions

How accurate is an online vocal range test?

The note detection is accurate to the nearest semitone when you hold a clear, steady note: this test only accepts a note after it has been held for about 0.4 seconds with a confident pitch reading, which filters out noise and octave glitches. What varies is you: whether you warmed up, how hard you pushed, and whether you counted strained notes. The voice-type label is an estimate based on range alone.

Is my vocal range good?

There is no pass mark. The conventional ranges for each classical voice type span about two octaves, which is what written repertoire for that voice expects. Many popular songs sit within roughly an octave and a half. Singing those notes in tune, with a free and consistent tone, matters far more than adding extra notes at the edges.

Should I include falsetto or vocal fry?

For a voice-type estimate, count only notes you can sing with a clear, controlled tone. Vocal fry at the bottom and a thin falsetto squeak at the top extend your total range, but including them will push the voice-type estimate away from where your voice actually works. You can run the test a second time with them if you are curious about your total range.

Can I extend my vocal range?

Usually a little, mostly at the top. Regular, gentle practice with semi-occluded exercises (lip trills, humming, singing through a straw) and slow sirens helps you access and coordinate notes you already have. Your lowest notes are more limited by the size of your vocal folds. Never push through pain or hoarseness; if hoarseness lasts more than a couple of weeks, see a doctor.

Why does my range change from day to day?

Warm-up, time of day, hydration, tiredness, and illness all change what your voice can do. Voices often feel lower and rougher first thing in the morning and loosen up after use. Test after a five-minute warm-up and compare results across a few days rather than trusting a single run.

Is this vocal range test free, and does it record me?

It is free, with no signup or email. Your microphone audio is analysed in your browser to detect pitch and is never recorded or uploaded. Only the final note names appear in the share link if you choose to copy it.
