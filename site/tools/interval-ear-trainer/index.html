How to use this interval trainer

  • Pick a set of intervals. Start with 4ths & 5ths or 3rds; move to All within an octave once those feel easy. Custom lets you tick any intervals from the minor 2nd (m2) to the octave (P8).
  • Pick a direction. Ascending plays the lower note first, descending plays the higher note first, and harmonic plays both together.
  • Listen, then answer. Tap Replay (or press space) as often as you like. After each answer you see the notes that were played, and you can hear the interval again melodically or harmonically, or hear the interval you guessed for comparison.
  • Review the round. Every 10 questions you get a per-interval breakdown, so you know which intervals to focus on next.

Leave random starting note on. If every question starts on C, you can end up recognizing the second note's pitch rather than the distance between the two notes.

How to practice interval ear training

Interval ear training is learning to recognize the distance between two pitches by sound. A few habits make the practice pay off:

  1. Start with two intervals that sound very different. The perfect 4th and perfect 5th are a good first pair. Add a new interval only once you're consistently right on the current set. Too many options at once makes everything sound alike.
  2. Harmonic first, then melodic. Hearing two notes together lets you focus on the color of the blend. Melodic intervals add the shape of the movement; train ascending first, then descending on its own.
  3. Sing it back. After each answer, hum both notes. Singing ties the sound to something you produce yourself, which tends to stick better than passive listening. More on this in why singing helps ear training.
  4. Short, frequent sessions. A few rounds a day beats one long session a week. Spacing practice out is one of the best-supported findings in learning research; see spaced repetition for musicians.
  5. Mix things up once the basics hold. Mixing intervals and directions within a session (rather than drilling one at a time) feels harder but tends to produce more durable learning. See interleaved practice.

When the trainer shows you the notes it played, they are spelled to match the interval: a major 3rd above E♭ reads E♭–G, and a minor 3rd above F♯ reads F♯–A.

Why song cues only get you partway

Song cues are a good on-ramp: when you hear two notes, you run through your list and find the tune that matches. The catch is that they're built for isolated two-note tests. In real music the same interval does a different job depending on the key around it, and nobody has time to hum Greensleeves mid-phrase. Over time the goal is to hear intervals as movements between notes of a key: "the 5th of the scale going up to the tonic", rather than "a fourth". Frequently asked questions

What is interval ear training?

Practice at recognizing the distance between two notes by sound alone: you hear two pitches and name the interval, such as a perfect fifth or a minor third. It's a core skill in aural-skills courses because melodies and chords are built from intervals.

Which intervals should I learn first?

Start with a small set that sounds very different, like the perfect 4th and perfect 5th, and only add more once you're consistently right. Thirds (major vs minor) make a good second step. Training all twelve intervals at once is the slowest way to begin, because too many options sound alike.

What's the difference between harmonic and melodic intervals?

A harmonic interval is two notes played at the same time, so you hear their blend. A melodic interval is two notes played one after the other, either ascending (low then high) or descending (high then low). They're heard differently, so it's worth practicing each.

Should I practice ascending and descending intervals separately?

Yes. Most learners find descending intervals harder, and recognizing an interval going up doesn't automatically mean you'll recognize it going down. Get comfortable with one direction, then train the other on its own before mixing them.

Do song references actually work for learning intervals?

They're a useful starting point, because a familiar tune gives you something to compare against. Their limit is that they work on isolated two-note tests and transfer poorly to real music, where hearing notes relative to a key matters more. Treat them as training wheels.
